Rectolejeunea ocellata Herzog, Trans. Proc. Roy. Soc. New Zealand 77: 255 (1949); Microlejeunea
ocellata (Herzog) Grolle, Haussknechtia 8: 60 (2001)
Type: NEW ZEALAND, North Island, near Wairoa, Hodgson s.n., 21 Jan 1934; holotype ?JE,
not seen.
Description: This species was described in detail by Schuster (2000b) in describing a new
monotypic subgenus Notholejeunea of Rectolejeunea, for which R. ocellata was the type.
Illustrations: Schuster (2000b, Figure 1).
Habitat: Epiphytic on tree trunks and roots in low-elevation forest and rainforest; also on
rotting wood.
Distribution on LHI: Known only from the summit of Transit Hill.
Total distribution: Australia (NSW, LHI), New Zealand.
Discussion: This species was not previously reported from Lord Howe Island. Schuster (2000b)
noted that it is very rare in New Zealand, being known at that time from only two collections
from North Island. Since then the species has been found in eastern New South Wales by Judith
Curnow, Tamas Pócs and Matt Renner (AVH 2018). Its presence on Lord Howe Island provides
a geographical link in its trans-Tasman distribution.
Grolle (2001) transferred Rectolejeunea ocellata to Microlejeunea, but this transfer has been
overlooked or ignored (e.g. McCarthy 2006, Renner and de Lange 2013). Because Grolle’s
circumscription of Microlejeunea is widely accepted, as discussed above, I can see no reason
not to accept the transfer.

This large family includes s large number of genera known from eastern Australia, New

Cuspidatula monodon (Taylor ex Lehm.) Steph.
Jungermannia monodon Taylor ex Lehm., Nov. Stirp. Pug. 8: 7 (April 1844); Jungermannia monodon
Hook.f. & Taylor, London J. Bot. 3: 559 (November 1844) nom. illeg.; Anastrophyllum monodon
(Hook.f. & Taylor) Steph., Hedwigia 1893: 140 (1893); Cuspidatula monodon (Hook.f. & Taylor) Steph.,
Sp. Hepat. 2: 126 (1901); Jamesoniella monodon (Hook.f. & Taylor) N.Kitag, Bull. Nara Univ. Educ. 30:
47 (1981).
Type: AUSTRALIA, Tasmania, ‘Crescit in Terra van Diemen caespites densos formans, attulit
Dr. Spence 1824. (Hb. Greville et Taylor.)’, Spence s.n., 1824, E-2414!d
Description: This species has been described in detail by Scott (1985) and Engel and Glenny
(2008).
Illustrations: Scott (1985, Figures 75, 76); Engel and Glenny (2008, Figures 158–160).
Habitat: Soil, rotting wood, tree trunks and rarely rock, in moist or humid situations.
Distribution on LHI: Eastern slopes of Mt Lidgbird.
Total distribution: Australia (Tas, Vic, NSW, Qld, LHI), New Zealand.
Discussion: Cuspidatula monodon was originally described by Lehmann (1844) as
‘Jungermannia monodon Taylor Ms.’, no doubt referring to the manuscript published
subsequently in the London Journal of Botany. Taylor published Jungermannia monodon as a
new species with a different type to that specified by Lehmann (from New Zealand, presumably
collected by J.D. Hooker or William Colenso in 1841), so his J. monodon must be considered
illegitimate as a later homonym of J. monodon Taylor ex Lehm. (The dates of publication cited
above are taken from Stafleu and Cowan (1979–2009)). The name Jungermannia monodon is
sometimes ascribed to ‘Taylor ex Lehm. & Lindenb.’, but Lehmann was the sole author of the
nine volumes of Pugillus published between 1828 and 1857.
Stephani (1893) republished the species in Anastrophyllum, and subsequently transferred it to
his new genus Cuspidatula (Stephani 1901). It was first reported from Lord Howe Island by
Stephani and Watts (1914).
The type has shortly acuminate leaf apices, as in the Lord Howe Island plants. Almost all
specimens identified as C. monodon from southern Australia (Tasmania, Victoria) seen for this
study have a similarly short acumen, consisting of quadrate to shortly rectangular cells (except
an elongate-triangular apical cell).
However, Engel and Glenny (2008) described C. monodon as having a long piliferous apex
consisting of 8–15 elongate (to 8 × 1) cells. The New Zealand material therefore did not appear
to agree with Cuspidatula monodon s.s. Furthermore, a specimen collected by myself recently
in northern Queensland, while similar to the New Zealand material as described by Engel and
Glenny (2008), differs in having the walls between the cells of the acumen distinctly bulging
(hardly or not bulging in New Zealand material) and in having the lobule of the male bracts
armed with two diverging teeth and no accessory armament (one tooth parallel to the lobule

margin, and an accessory tooth or flange in New Zealand material). Therakyll (2014) noted two
types of Tasmanian plant, one with a very short acumen and up to 18 oil bodies per cell
(agreeing with C. monodon s.s.), and another with an elongate acumen and more numerous oil
bodies (agreeing with New Zealand material). However, a review by David Glenny (Landcare
Research New Zealand, pers. comm. August 2015) found more variability in the species than
reported by Engel & Glenny (2008), and concluded that there was no difference between
Tasmanian and New Zealand plants. Although Lord Howe Island plants have massive, bulging
trigones that are often confluent, and only 3 or 4 large, dark oil bodies per cell, this falls within
the range of variation reported for the species.


Schistochilaceae H.Buch.
The family Schistochilaceae is characterised largely by complicate-bilobed leaves in which a
keel forms the junction between a large ventral (postical) lobe and a smaller dorsal (antical)
lobe, dark crimson to purple rhizoids — not magenta as stated by He et al. (2014) — and the
development of a shoot-calyptra in a coelocaule, enclosing a shortly cylindrical capsule.
The family has undergone considerable taxonomic change in recent years, particularly because
genera created on morphological grounds were found to be unsupportable in genetic analyses
(He and Glenny 2010, He and Sun 2013, Sun et al. 2014). Following the broad concept of He
and Glenny (2010) and He and Sun (2013), the Schistochilaceae is here considered to include a
single genus, Schistochila. This genus was not previously reported from Lord Howe Island.
