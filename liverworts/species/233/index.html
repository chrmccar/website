William Mitten (1882) listed 16 mosses from Lord Howe Island in his catalogue of Australian mosses, comprising the nine species newly described by Hampe and the seven mosses listed in Mitten (1873a). In 1892 a list of 20 mosses collected on the island by Thomas Whitelegge in 1887 and identified by Viktor Brotherus were listed in the Proceedings of the Linnean Society of New South Wales as a result of their being exhibited by Whitelegge at a meeting of the society (Anon. 1892). Seven of these were new species but were published without descriptions. Only two of these seven were subsequently published with descriptions by Brotherus, leaving the other five as nomina nuda with their identities unresolved. In 1901 J.B. Förster described a new species, Rhacomitrium leptostomoides J.B.Först., from Lord Howe Island, but the identity has been the subject of much uncertainty (see the discussion in the taxonomic section on Grimmiaceae). In 1914 Franz Stephani and William Watts published a number of new species and new records of liverworts for Australia, including 31 species from Lord Howe of which seven were new species (Stephani and Watts 1914). These records were based entirely on collections made by Watts in 1911. In the following year Viktor Brotherus and Watts published a treatment of the mosses from Lord Howe Island, in which they enumerated 92 species, including 22 new species, based mostly on the 1911 collections of Watts (Brotherus and Watts 1915). Scott and Stone (1976) used this list as the basis for almost all of the species they reported from the island, and Miller et al. (1978) followed suit. Touw and Falter-van den Haak (1989) added Thuidiopsis sparsa (Hook.f. & Wilson) Broth. to the island’s flora. Ramsay (1984) summarised the then known moss flora of Lord Howe Island, based on literature records and named specimens held in the National Herbarium of New South Wales. She reported 105 species in 36 families, of which 21 species were considered endemic. With some additions and corrections, this was the basis for the records from Lord Howe Island listed by Streimann and Curnow (1989). Hattori (1987) reviewed the Frullania species of Lord Howe Island, accepting six species including three new ones. However, each of these species differs from C. elizabethae in at least two significant characters. Cololejeunea elizabethae perhaps most closely resembles C. angulata (Steph.) Mizutani from New Guinea 197 and the Philippines, which also has large and often doubled pearl-like intermediate thickenings in the walls of the lobe cells, as well as nodulose cells in the lobule and ovate-oblong gynoecial bracts (Mizutani 1965). However, in that species the lobule has one tooth, the trigones are small, and the hyaline border is 1–2 cells wide. A plant figured as C. cuneata (Lehm. & Lindenb.) Herzog by Herzog (1947: Fig. 6) from the Comoro Islands is also similar, but it has only two long, curved teeth on the distal end of the lobule, the outer chlorophyllose cells of the leaves are thin-walled and the cells of the lobules are not nodulose. (This plant is clearly not Jungermannia cuneata as described by Lehmann and Lindenberg (in Lehmann 1832: 56), since that species has only one tooth on the lobule and does not have such large trigones and intermediate thickenings (Tixier 1986: 48). The species illustrated by H erzog is in fact Cololejeunea marginata (Lehm. & Lindenb.) Pearson, a species widespread in the East African islands.) The common and widespread Cololejeunea lanciloba Steph. has small trigones with small intermediate thickenings, the lobules are commonly explanate, and the gynoecial bracts are similar in shape to the leaves and have a continuous border of hyaline cells (Mizutani 1961). C. elizabethae is the only species of Cololejeunea in the region that grows on rock; all other species are epiphytic or epiphyllous. Compared to Cololejeunea laevigata, C. elizabethae has a much different appearance in the field. In C. laevigata the leaves are turned slightly to the dorsal side and not flattened, and the colour is a darker and more opaque olive green. It is immediately distinct microscopically from C. elizabethae in lacking a border of hyaline cells on the leaves and gynoecial bracts. Other differences in C. laevigata are leaves much more variable in size and generally smaller, lobule teeth consisting of only 1 or 2 cells, mid-lamina cells mostly isodiametric or only slightly elongate, intermediate thickenings much smaller and rarely multiple, lobule cells not or hardly nodulose, and the perianth with distinctly auriculate wings. The boundaries of the genus have been very fluid in recent times as a result of the application of molecular methods to taxonomy, and species are still being taken from and added to it. Lejeunea has not been monographed for Australia or any other nearby region.
