Discussion: Chiloscyphus semiteres must be the most common leafy liverwort in Australasia,
and the variety semiteres must be the most common variety. Several collections of this species
consisted of depauperate plants with reduced armature on the underleaves and often slightly
retuse leaf margins. However, these were fertile and could be identified easily because of the
distinctive perianth.
David Long annotated E-2699 as ‘may be an isotype of Jungermannia semiteres Lehmann’.
There can be little doubt that it is, since it is also annotated ‘Cape of Good Hope, leg. Ecklon
per Lehmann, 1830’. A specimen in G (G-51447) is annotated ‘type’, but there is no
information about the collector in the packet, so it cannot be accepted as type material.

Geocalycaceae De Not.
Although no longer including Chiloscyphus and Lophocolea, Geocalycaceae is still a large
family, containing at least 25 genera and over 200 species. Two genera occur on Lord Howe
Island.

Geocalyx Nees
Seven extant species are attributed to this genus, but it has not been monographed and I think,
from my reading of the original descriptions, that some species are synonymous or belong to
other genera. Scott (1985) already noted that G. novae-zelandiae is a synonym of
G. caledonicus.

Geocalyx caledonicus Steph.
Geocalyx caledonicus Steph., Sp. Hepat. 3: 265 (1908)
Type: NEW CALEDONIA: Balansa s.n., without date, G not seen.
Description: This species has been described by Allison and Child (1975) and Scott (1985).
Illustration: Also Scott (1985, Figure 78).
Habitat: Rotting wood.
Distribution on LHI: Known only from the eastern slopes of Mt Lidgbird.

Total distribution: Australia (Tas to tropical Qld), New Caledonia, New Zealand.
Discussion: This species has not been reported previously from Lord Howe Island, possibly
because its two locations there are difficult to reach. Its presence on the island is not surprising,
since it is known from New Caledonia, eastern Australia and New Zealand. Scott (1985) stated
that the oil bodies take up much of the cell area, which might give the impression that they
dominate the cell. However, in the Lord Howe Island material at least, the oil bodies rarely take
up more than half of the cell area in plane view.
Unlike its Northern Hemisphere congener Geocalyx graveolens (Schrad.) Nees, which is called
Turpswort in Britain on account of its pungent odour when crushed fresh (Atherton et al. 2010),
G. caledonicus has no odour.

    T dispar (V) is rather dull olive-brown. It is common on soil in wet sclerophyll
forest; and occasional in other damp habitats. The wedge-shaped leaves are 4-fid on
the main stems but only 3-fid on the branches. The underleaves are 3-fid or 4-fid
on the main stems, but mostly 2-fid on the branches. T patentissima (TV) is very
similar to T centipes, but has 3-fid underleaves. T herzogii (TV) is a minute plant
in which the leaves and underleaves are reduced to a pair of long, spine-like lobes.
It is quite common in tree-fern gullies in wet forest and rainforest, creeping
through other bryophytes, but it is probably commonly overlooked because of its
size.

Similar species: Lepidozia glaucophylla, Leptophyllopsis laxa

In the field the differences between this genus and Heteroscyphus are not easily defined.
In Chiloscyphus at least one leaf in each pair is not joined to the corresponding
underleaf, but in Heteroscyphus both leaves are usually joined.

Similar species: *Geocalyx caledonicus, Heteroscyphus spp., Lethocolea pansa, Jungermannia
spp., *Pedinophyllum monoicum, Plagiochila spp.

C. latifoliue Lophocoleaceae                                                    WSTVAN
The shoots of this spec1es (for merly Lophocolea
bidentata) are green to straw-coloured, forming loose wefts
often creeping through other bryophytes in grasslands and
grassy understoreys. The leaves have two long, narrow
spines at the leaf apex, often curved away from each other. The
dorsal margin is more or less straight, but the ventral margin is
distinctly rounded. The underleaves are deeply bifid to
beyond half-way, often with long extra teeth.
     C. biciliatus (TV), a mainly forest species, has leaves
with shorter apical spines and curved margins.

C. muricatue Lophocoleaceae
                                                                                  STVAN
This is a common but overlooked species in wet forests, where
it grows on bark, rotting wood, and sometimes leaves and fern
fronds. The leaves are mostly bilobed and covered in spike-
like papillae on the dorsal leaf surface, and there are scattered
papillae on the ventral surface. The leaves thus resemble
minute, rough tongues. The colour is usually deep green, with        C. muricatus {leaf in

C. echinellus (TV) also has papillose leaves, but the stem is
visible between the leaves and is also papillose, and the leaf
margins are irregularly toothed.
