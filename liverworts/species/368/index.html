Recognition: Radula aneurysmalis is distinct in its extremely small size, with shoots less than 350 pm wide, and in its subequal lobes and lobules. Radula aneurysmalis is superficially similar to smaller members of the Lejeuneaceae, such as Drepanolejeunea and Harpalejeunea , however is immediately identified by its I) lack of underleaves, 2) production of rhizoids on the ventral surface of the lobule, never on the stem, 3) the single large oil body per lobe cell, and 4) the dorso-ventrally flattened peri- anths, that are never inflated. Radula aneurysmalis can be confused with R. physoloba, as extensive growth of mi- crophyllous branches can occur in R. physoloba, particularly when plants of the latter grow above tree line. However, these branches can always be seen to originate from the large full sized axes they grow with, and they never produce antheridia or gynoecia. Production of reproductive organs from microphyllous branches is indicative of R. aneurysmalis. Radula aneurysmalis can also be distinguished from microphyllous axes of R. physoloba by a number of features which are summarised in Table I. Radula aneUly smalis bears a particular resemblance to R. compacta, best expressed in the form of the lobules preceding the gynoecia. These two species are separable only by the persistent microphyllous habit of R. aneurysmalis, and the association of microphyllous axes with axes of normal stature in R. compacta. Notes: While Schuster (1980b) uses the spelling R. aneurismalis, we follow the spelling presented in Taylor's protologue. Rodway (1916) was of the opinion that R. tas- manica was a well developed form of R. aneurysmalis. Most likely Rodway was confusing R. tasmanica with R. physoloba or R. compacta (see under R. tasmanica below) as differ- ences in the oil-bodies between these two species (see below) clearly indicate that this can- not be the case, besides which R. tasmanica never produces microphyllous branches. As pointed out above, this species does bear a marked resemblance to the microphyllous axes of R. physoloba, and an even closer resemblance, particularly in the form of the lobule on leaves preceding the production of gynoecia, to R. compacta. Johnson 14141, 14142, 14 Feb 1973 (WELT); Campbell Island, A. M. Bailey 1633, Jan 1959 (CRR). Radula aneurismalis is a puzzling little known species and there is some confusion 154 1. Hattori Bot. Lab. No. 98 2 005 about its exact identity. When Hooker & Taylor (1845) described it, both male and female plants were mentioned. However, Stephani's Icon n. 8755 only showed the male plants which consist of two slender shoots with globular leaves, resembling those of R. cavifolia. Radula cavi{olia was synonymized by Castle (1963) with R. aneurismalis, based perhaps on similarities of the globular leaf lobes in the male plants, but Yamada (1984) doubted such placement. There was no indication that Castle (\963), while listing numerous speci- mens of R. cav!{olia from various regions of SE Asia, had seen the female plants of R. aneurismalis. The figure of R. aneurismalis in Castle (1963: fig. 12) is a composite of both species, from the male plant of R. aneurismalis and the female plant of R. cavifolia. An ex- amination of the type material of both species confirmed Yamada's opinion (Fig. Ic, 8b). Radula cavifolia, common in various parts of Asia, is among the smallest in the genus, measuring not more than \ mm wide with leaves (usually 0.6- 0.8 mm). The leaves are al- most globular and the lobules almost half the size of the lobes. The perianths (Fig. 8b) have a long and narrow base and quite different from that in R. aneurismalis (Fig. Id, f). The type material in FH only consists of male plants about 1.2 mm wide with leaves (shown here in Fig. Ic). As commented by Hooker & Taylor (1845), the perianth is large compared to the size of the plants. The leaves are closely imbricate with broad lobules con- tinuous with the dorsal margin of the leaf lobe. Castle (1963) described Radula compacta as a new species and the material listed under R. compacta were all previously identified by Rodway as R. aneurismalis (Fig. I f). An examination of the large collections of Radula located in HO shows that some col- lections identified as R. aneurismalis belong to R. tasmanica and those labelled as R. physoloba actually belong to R. aneurismalis . As drawn by Castle (1963) and Stephani (1985), it was originally thought that R. aneurismalis is a very tiny species, resembling members of Lejeuneaceae. A detailed examination of all these collections reveals some- thing quite startling. Only two collections consist of smal1leaves and amentula with globu- lar leaves, typical of the male plants of R. aneurismalis. Most collections have larger leaves similar to the type of R. compacta (plants 1.5 mm wide with leaves), bearing normal branches and leaves, while some collections have normal shoots bearing both normal branches and very small amentula with globular leaves (Fig. I f). Some collections (notably HO-305593, HO-672l9) lack the normal branches altogether but are composed almost en- tirely of amentula-like branches (0.5 mm wide with leaves) bearing only globular leaves, apparently in a perpetual vegetative state, giving the plants a powdery appearance in the dry condition (Fig. 2c). Some light can be found in Rodway's (1916) comment about R. aneurismalis: "I have specimens in which the ends of the shoots of typical R. aneurysmalis have grown into typical forms of R. tasmanica .. ." The mystery of the apparent polymorphic nature of this species can now be solved. Figure le here probably shows what Rodway had seen where tiny shoots of R. aneurismalis grow into larger normal shoots typical of "R. compacta." A few collections even have amentula bearing clusters of very small crowded leaves (Fig. 2d), apparently subjected to the same conditions found in R. colensoi, to be discussed under R. physoloba . 3.
